Learning route and prerequisites

Start with a decision that matters: an agent must act using incomplete information, limited computation and an objective that someone has chosen. This book develops several ways to specify that problem. Learning estimates patterns from data; search compares sequences of actions; decision processes combine actions with uncertain outcomes; games add other decision makers; graphical models represent uncertain variables; logic states constraints and draws consequences. The final chapters use those distinctions to examine language models and their place in society.

The route below describes the full twenty-recording scope. The chapter index gives the current availability and validation status of each chapter. A topic's presence in this route does not mean its chapter has completed review.

What to bring

  • Linear algebra: vectors, matrix multiplication, dot products and dimensions. When an array has several axes, distinguish the meaning of an axis from its length.
  • Calculus: derivatives, partial derivatives and the chain rule. Be able to differentiate a finite sum and identify which quantities are held fixed.
  • Probability: finite distributions, conditional probability, expectation and independence. Conditional probabilities need a conditioning event with positive probability; a sample and its expectation are different objects.
  • Programming: functions, lists, dictionaries, loops and exceptions in Python. Read a small program's inputs, outputs and failure conditions before running it. Dependency requirements are stated beside individual programs.
  • Proof habits: follow an implication, test a boundary case and distinguish a counterexample from a general theorem. Each chapter states its additional assumptions locally.

The first pass: build the models in order

  1. Chapters 1–4: objectives and learning. Begin with expected loss and tensor axes, then move through regression, classification and neural-network training. Keep the data distribution, prediction rule, objective and optimizer separate. Before moving on, derive an update on a small example and explain why improving a training objective need not improve every evaluation metric.
  2. Chapters 5–6: search. Specify a state, admissible actions, transition rules and path costs. Compare exhaustive enumeration, dynamic programming, uniform-cost search and A*. Check the graph and heuristic assumptions before claiming an optimal answer. The learning chapters explain where an estimate can come from; the search chapters explain what an algorithm is allowed to infer from it.
  3. Chapters 7–9: decisions and feedback. Replace a single predicted successor with a distribution of outcomes. Define rewards, discount, policies and terminal behavior before evaluating or improving a policy. Move from a known model to sampled experience, then from a table to parameterized values and policies. Identify the difference between an expected backup, a sampled target and a gradient estimate.
  4. Chapters 10–11: games. Add move order, information and another player's objective. Connect search to minimax and pruning, then connect learning to approximate game values. For simultaneous decisions, distinguish an announced probability distribution from its revealed sampled action. A zero-sum security guarantee and a general-game Nash equilibrium answer different questions.
  5. Chapters 12–14: probabilistic inference and learning. Represent a joint distribution by a graph and local conditional factors. Separate a causal intervention from conditioning on an observation. Compare exact inference with sampling, then estimate parameters when some variables are hidden. Check normalization, support and identifiability before interpreting a fitted model.
  6. Chapters 15–16: logic. Specify interpretations, satisfaction and entailment. Distinguish what a formula means from how an inference procedure searches for a conclusion. Moving to first-order logic adds variables, quantifiers and unification; the soundness and completeness of a procedure require their own stated scope.
  7. Chapter 17: language models. Revisit conditional prediction, representations and optimization for sequences. Track tokenization, context, computational work and storage separately. Relate inference-time search and reinforcement learning to the earlier models without assuming that a language model automatically satisfies every earlier theorem's hypotheses.
  8. Chapters 18–20: consequences and research judgment. Examine who supplies data and computation, who bears costs, what evaluations measure and what remains uncertain. Distinguish output quality, transparency, causal evidence, economic measurement and social value. Finish by selecting a consequential question and designing a first experiment whose possible outcomes would change what you believe.

The second pass: connect assumptions to failures

Return to an example you have already computed and change one assumption. What happens when two histories share the same recorded state but have different futures? When a terminal value is stale? When a heuristic is inconsistent? When a sampler cannot move between parts of the support? When a measured proxy rewards the wrong behavior? Recompute the small case before making a broad claim. These failures connect the chapters more usefully than memorizing a list of algorithm names.

Work with the programs and exercises

Read a chapter's model and implementation contract first. Predict one output by hand, run its original program, and compare the result with the displayed output. Then change an input within the documented domain and explain the change. Try a meaningful invalid input and check that the stated failure occurs. Solve each exercise before expanding its solution; a complete solution should show the reasoning, not only the final number.

Consult the numerical lab's stated availability and validation status before using its teaching experiments and reproducible computations. Finite examples illustrate mechanisms and expose counterexamples; they do not certify deployment safety, statistical generalization or an asymptotic theorem.
